# The Virgin of Isaiah 7:14

Worked example for case [`isa-7-14-almah-parthenos`](../mistranslations.json). Generated by `tools/build_study.py` — every table below is computed from this repository's version files.

> **Does the title "Virgin Mary" rest on a mistranslation?**

Short answer: no — but a famous *prophecy* does.

The question conflates two different texts written in two different languages, and separating them settles most of the argument before any Hebrew is examined.

| | Text | Original language | Translation dispute? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The prophecy | Isaiah 7:14 | **Hebrew** | **Yes** — this is the famous one |
| The narrative | Luke 1:27, 1:34; Matthew 1:18–25 | **Greek** | No |

Luke was *written* in Greek. There is no Hebrew original behind it. Whatever Isaiah meant in the 8th century BCE, it does not change what Luke wrote in the 1st century CE.

Luke 1:34 is the decisive line. Mary's `ἄνδρα οὐ γινώσκω` — "I do not know a man" — is the author's own idiom in the author's own language, and Young's Literal renders it flatly: *"seeing a husband I do not know."* No translator stands between the reader and that claim.

So the title **Virgin Mary comes from Luke's Greek, not from Isaiah's Hebrew.** What the translation shift produced is the *proof-text* — the claim that the Hebrew Bible foretold a virgin birth. That claim is what the rest of this study examines.

A literal rendering:

> Therefore he will give — the Lord himself — to you (plural) a sign: **Look, the young woman is pregnant and giving birth to a son, and she shall call his name Immanu-El.**

Three features the smooth English versions flatten:

1. **`hā-'almāh` is definite.** "*The* young woman" — someone Isaiah could point at, not "a virgin" in the abstract.
2. **`hārāh` is a stative, not a future.** She is already pregnant as Isaiah speaks. The NET and NRSV preserve this; the KJV's "shall conceive" does not.
3. **`lākhem` is plural.** The sign is addressed to the dynasty, not to the king personally.

## 3. What the sign is for

**Isaiah 7:14 does not stand alone — verses 15 and 16 say what the sign measures**

| Reference | Version | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isaiah 7:14 | `NEW REVISED STANDARD VERSION` | Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Look, the young woman is with child and shall bear a son, and shall name him Immanuel. |
| Isaiah 7:15 | `NEW REVISED STANDARD VERSION` | He shall eat curds and honey by the time he knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good. |
| Isaiah 7:16 | `NEW REVISED STANDARD VERSION` | For before the child knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good, the land before whose two kings you are in dread will be deserted. |

The sign is a **military timetable**. A child is born now; before he is old enough to tell right from wrong, the two kings menacing Judah will be gone. This is the Syro-Ephraimite crisis of the 730s BCE, and Ahaz is being told how long he has to wait.

A child conceived seven centuries later cannot function as a sign to Ahaz about a war in his own lifetime. That, rather than the lexicography, is the strongest argument that Isaiah 7:14 is not about Mary — and it does not depend on what `'almāh` means at all.

Seven occurrences, and not one of them requires virginity. `'almāh` marks **age and social status** — a young woman of marriageable age — not sexual history.

Two entries carry most of the weight, and they cut in opposite directions:

Verse 16 establishes Rebekah's virginity. To do it, the text needs a **different word** — `bethulah`, the dedicated term — *and* an explicit clause: "neither had any man known her." Only later, at verse 43, is she an `'almāh`.

That is the shape of the evidence. `'almāh` is compatible with virginity; it does not assert it. Where the Hebrew Bible wants to assert it, it reaches for other words.

## 6. The Septuagint translators were not consistent — and that is the finding

**How the Greek Old Testament rendered ʿalmāh at each of the seven places**

| Reference | `SWETE'S SEPTUAGINT` uses | Text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genesis 24:43 | **virgin (parthenos)** | ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ ἕστηκα ἐπὶ τὴν πηγὴν τοῦ ὕδατος, καὶ αἱ θυγατέρες τῶν ἀνθρώπων τῆς πόλεως … |
| Exodus 2:8 | **young woman (neanis)** | ἡ δὲ εἶπεν ἡ θυγάτηρ Φαραώ Πορεύου. ἐλθοῦσα δὲ ἡ νεᾶνις ἐκάλεσεν τὴν μητέρα τοῦ παιδίου. |
| Psalms 68:25 | **young woman (neanis)** | προέφθασαν ἄρχοντες ἐχόμενοι ψαλλόντων, ἐν μέσῳ νεανίδων τυμπανιστριῶν. |
| Proverbs 30:19 | **neither** | βασιλεὺς δίκαιος ἀνίστησιν χώραν, ἀνὴρ δὲ παράνομος κατασκάπτει. |
| Song of Solomon 1:3 | **young woman (neanis)** | καὶ ὀσμὴ μύρων σου ὑπὲρ πάντα τὰ ἀρώματα· μύρον ἐκκενωθὲν ὄνομά σου. διὰ τοῦτο νεάνιδες … |
| Song of Solomon 6:8 | **young woman (neanis)** | ἑξήκοντά εἰσιν βασίλισσαι, καὶ ὀγδοήκοντα παλλακαί, καὶ νεάνιδες ὧν οὐκ ἔστιν ἀριθμός· |
| Isaiah 7:14 | **virgin (parthenos)** | διὰ τοῦτο δώσει Κύριος αὐτὸς ὑμῖν σημεῖον· ἰδοὺ ἡ παρθένος ἐν γαστρὶ λήμψεται καὶ τέξεται … |

The same translator used 2 different equivalents for one source word: **virgin (parthenos)**, **young woman (neanis)**. The choice at each verse was a decision, not a default.

This is the single most useful result in the study, and it is computed rather than asserted.

The Septuagint translators had `νεᾶνις` (*neanis*, "young woman") available and **used it four times** for `'almāh`. At Isaiah 7:14 they chose `παρθένος` (*parthenos*, "virgin") instead.

That forecloses the two most common ways of arguing about this verse:

- It was **not a lexical accident**. A translator who writes *neanis* at Exodus 2:8 and Song 6:8 knows the word.
- It was **not a Christian invention**. The Septuagint is a pre-Christian Jewish translation, made two centuries before Jesus, with no stake in the outcome.

What happened at Isaiah 7:14 was an **interpretive decision** by a Jewish translator working in Alexandria. Matthew inherited it; the Vulgate hardened it into *virgo*; English inherited that.

*One caveat visible in the table above:* Proverbs is heavily reordered in the Greek, so the reference does not align and the row shows no match. That is a versification artifact, not evidence. Genesis 24:16 — where the Hebrew has `bethulah` — reads `παρθένος` and then still adds "a man had not known her," which suggests even *parthenos* was felt to need reinforcement.

Matthew's `ἡ παρθένος` is lifted from the Septuagint, essentially verbatim. For Matthew 1:23, "virgin" is the **correct** translation of what Matthew wrote. His argument works in the language he wrote it in.

The mistranslation, if that is the word, happened around 200 BCE in Greek — not in the 1st century CE, and not in 1611.

## 9. What scholars actually accept

| Position | View of Isaiah 7:14 | Representative version here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical / academic | `'almāh` = "young woman"; the sign concerns Ahaz's lifetime. Majority view across confessional lines. | `NEW REVISED STANDARD VERSION`, `NET BIBLE` |
| Jewish | "Young woman," unanimously; the verse has never been messianic in Jewish tradition. | `JPS TANAKH 1917` |
| Catholic | Accepts the philology, keeps the doctrine — which rests on Luke and Matthew and on tradition, not on Isaiah's Hebrew. | `NEW AMERICAN BIBLE` |
| Conservative evangelical | A real minority defends "virgin": `'almāh` is never demonstrably used of a non-virgin, the pre-Christian LXX read it as *parthenos*, and prophecy may have dual fulfilment. | `ENGLISH STANDARD VERSION`, `NEW INTERNATIONAL VERSION` |

**Everyone agrees** that Matthew was quoting the Greek and that "virgin" is right for Matthew 1:23.

So the honest verdict, and the one recorded in the dataset as consensus `majority` rather than `strong`:

> "Young woman" is the better rendering of `'almāh`. The Septuagint's `parthenos` was an interpretive narrowing, not an error of competence, and it was made by Jewish translators before Christianity existed. The virgin birth stands on Luke's Greek either way. What the shift produced is a **prophecy** — the claim that the Hebrew Bible foretold a virgin birth — and that claim does not survive contact with the Hebrew.

The collateral damage was real. This verse was a fixture of medieval Christian–Jewish disputations, where Jewish scholars pointing at `'almāh` were accused of corrupting their own scriptures. When the RSV printed "young woman" in 1952, copies were burned from American pulpits, and the controversy fed directly into the founding of the translation projects that became the NASB and NIV.

This study is generated from `studies/isa-7-14-almah.json` by `tools/build_study.py`. Every table is computed; only the `prose` blocks are written by hand. To build another one, copy the spec and change the terms.

| Block | What it answers | Reused for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `contrast` | Is the doctrine actually resting on this verse? | Separating a claim from its proof-text — the move that does the most work here |
| `interlinear` | What does the source literally say? | Any case where word order, definiteness or verb aspect is flattened in translation |
| `concordance` | How is this word used elsewhere? (surface forms) | Terms with no Strong's number, or cross-version spelling checks |
| `strongs` | The same question, lemma-accurate | `G733` arsenokoitai (2×), `G831` authentein (1×), `G1249` diakonos (28×), `H7585` sheol, `H5828` ezer |
| `renderings` | Did the translator have alternatives, and use them elsewhere? | Any source→target pair in the corpus: Hebrew→LXX, Greek→Vulgate |
| `verse_table` | Where did the versions land? | Every case |

The `strongs` and `renderings` blocks are the ones worth generalising. Together they answer the question that decides most of these cases: **did the translator have a choice, and what did they do with it elsewhere?** A translator who used two different equivalents for one source word was making decisions, and the decision at the disputed verse is the thing to explain.

Two guardrails are built into the builder. `expect_kept` pins the number of genuine occurrences, so if the corpus changes the build fails rather than quietly reporting a different number. `exclusions` requires every filtered hit to carry a stated reason, printed below the table — the seven `'almāh` occurrences here are what remains after seven homographs are excluded *in public*.

**Known limits.** Surface-form matching is not lemmatisation: it misses suppletive forms and catches homographs, which is why `exclusions` exists and why the `strongs` block is preferred where a number is available. Reference alignment between the Hebrew and the Septuagint still fails wherever the Greek reorders material, as Proverbs 30:19 shows above - the Septuagint has no word-level tagging in this corpus, so that one is not yet fixable here.
